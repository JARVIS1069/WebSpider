The Quiet Architecture of Spider Silk

Spider silk is one of the most remarkable materials found in nature. Weight for weight, a strand of dragline silk is stronger than steel, yet it is also elastic enough to absorb the impact of a flying insect. Researchers have studied spider silk for decades, hoping to reproduce its properties in the laboratory.

An orb weaver can produce up to seven different kinds of silk, each with its own purpose. Some silk is sticky and used for catching prey, while other silk is dry and forms the structural frame of the web. The spider decides which gland to use depending on the job it is doing at that moment.

Kinds of silk

  • Dragline silk forms the frame and the safety line of the spider.
  • Capture silk is coated with glue droplets that trap insects.
  • Wrapping silk is used to swaddle prey for later consumption.
  • Egg sac silk protects the next generation of spiders from predators and weather.

How a web is built

The spider begins by releasing a thread into the wind. When the free end catches on a distant surface, the spider tightens the bridge line and walks across it, reinforcing the strand with a second thread. From this bridge it drops a Y-shaped frame, and the radial threads follow like the spokes of a wheel.

Finally the spider lays a sticky spiral from the outside inwards, using the radial threads as scaffolding. The whole process takes about an hour, and many spiders eat the old web each evening to recycle the proteins. Read more at Nature or ScienceDaily.

function webStrength(diameter, species) {
  const base = species === "orb" ? 1.3 : 0.9;
  return base * Math.sqrt(diameter);
}
